Lahori Chargha vs Sajji: What Is the Difference?

Quick answer: Lahori chargha is usually richly spiced, while Balochi sajji is known for simpler seasoning and roasting. Recipes vary by region and cook.

The seasoning philosophy is different

Lahori chargha is commonly associated with a bold marinade built from spices, ginger-garlic and tangy ingredients, though family recipes vary. Sajji is often linked with a simpler seasoning profile that lets the roasted meat remain central. These are useful traditions, not rigid rules: restaurants and home cooks adapt both dishes.

Chargha often uses more than one cooking stage

Many chargha methods marinate the chicken, partially cook or steam it, then fry, roast or finish it at high heat to develop colour. The exact sequence depends on the recipe. The goal is flavour through the meat and a well-coloured exterior without leaving the centre undercooked.

Sajji is closely associated with roasting

Balochi-style sajji is widely associated with roasting a whole or large portion of meat, often over fire or in a tandoor-style setup. Seasoning can be restrained or more elaborate depending on the cook. Because a whole bird cooks differently from cut pieces, heat control and checking the thickest part matter more than relying on a fixed time alone.

The finished textures can differ

Chargha may have a spiced, crisp or deeply coloured outer layer, while sajji often highlights roasted meat and a simpler exterior. Neither description guarantees one texture: equipment, marinade, cut size and cooking method all change the result.

Do not treat the names as interchangeable

A whole spiced chicken is not automatically chargha or sajji. Marinade style, cooking method and regional tradition all contribute to the identity of the dish, and restaurant versions may combine techniques.

Chargha vs sajji at a glance

Use this comparison as a practical guide rather than a strict rulebook; household and restaurant recipes vary across Pakistan.

Main flavourUsually a more pronounced spice-and-marinade flavourOften highlights the roasted meat with simpler seasoning
Typical preparationMarinate, then cook and finish for colour or crispnessRoast a whole bird or large portion with controlled, even heat
What to watchAvoid burning the outer spices while the centre finishes cookingAvoid drying the meat while ensuring the thickest part cooks through
Good choice whenYou want a boldly seasoned chicken dishYou want a roast-focused dish with a simpler flavour profile

Kitchen note: Cooking equipment and bird size affect timing more than the dish name does. Use a food thermometer and confirm the thickest part reaches 74°C (165°F); do not rely on skin colour alone.

Which one should you cook?

Choose chargha when you want a more assertively seasoned chicken dish and are happy to plan for marination and its cooking stages. Choose sajji when you want a roast-focused preparation and can manage the slower, even cooking of larger pieces. For either dish, use a food thermometer in the thickest part of the chicken; it should reach 74°C (165°F) for safety. If you choose chargha, our chicken marinating guide explains why marinade time and acidity matter; for the roast itself, follow the Chicken Sajji recipe method rather than assuming both dishes cook alike.

Common mistakes when comparing the two

  • Assuming every chargha recipe must be deep-fried or every sajji must use exactly the same seasoning.
  • Comparing a whole roasted bird with small fried pieces as if the cooking times were interchangeable.
  • Using colour alone to decide whether chicken is safely cooked.

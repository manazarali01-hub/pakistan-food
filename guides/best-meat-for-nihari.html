Best Meat for Nihari: Cuts That Stay Tender in Slow Cooking

Quick answer: Nihari benefits from beef or mutton cuts with connective tissue that soften during slow cooking. Shank is a classic choice because long cooking develops a rich broth and tender meat.

Which cut should you buy?

For a home pot of nihari, beef shank is a dependable first choice: it has connective tissue that softens during slow moist cooking, and that gelatin helps give the broth body. If shank is unavailable, ask the butcher for a slow-cooking beef cut such as chuck rather than a very lean steak cut.

At the butcher: Ask for nihari-size pieces from shank, with some bone-in pieces if you like a traditional presentation. Keep the pieces reasonably similar in size so they tenderise at a similar pace.

Shank, chuck, bone-in and boneless

  • Beef shank: a strong all-round choice for tender meat and a full-bodied broth after patient cooking.
  • Beef chuck or another stewing cut: a practical alternative when shank is unavailable; tenderness depends on the exact cut and piece size.
  • Bone-in pieces: can add flavour and a traditional presentation, but take care when serving around bones.
  • Boneless pieces: easier to portion and eat, though the broth may need more help from stock and careful reduction to reach the body you want.

Why very lean cuts can disappoint

Lean quick-cooking cuts are designed for different jobs. In a long simmer they can become dry or stringy before the broth develops the texture expected from nihari. Choose a cut that suits slow cooking, and let time—not a hard rolling boil—do the tenderising.

Piece size and doneness cues

Large shank pieces take longer to become tender than small boneless cubes. Ask for even pieces and start checking tenderness before assuming every pot will finish at the same time. A fork should enter easily and the meat should yield without feeling tight or rubbery.

Slow pot or pressure cooker?

A pressure cooker can shorten the tenderising stage, but timing varies with the appliance, amount of meat, cut and piece size. Follow your cooker manual, use its minimum liquid guidance, and release pressure safely before opening. If the meat is still firm, continue cooking rather than thickening the gravy too early.

Finish the gravy after the meat is tender

Let the beef become tender before thickening the gravy. If you add atta, whisk it with a little slightly cooled nihari liquid in a separate bowl, then stir it in gradually and simmer gently until the raw flour taste disappears.

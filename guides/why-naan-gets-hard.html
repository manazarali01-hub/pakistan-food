Why Does Naan Get Hard?

Quick answer: Hard naan usually comes from a dry dough, insufficient rest, too much flour during shaping, overcooking, or leaving the cooked bread exposed so steam escapes. Naan should cook quickly at high heat; a long dry bake turns a soft bread into a cracker.

Dry dough starts the problem early

A firm dough can be easy to handle but has less moisture available to create a soft crumb. Add liquid gradually while mixing and stop when the dough is soft and workable rather than stiff. Different flours absorb differently, so a fixed water number is only a starting point.

Rest allows the dough to relax

After kneading, cover the dough so its surface does not dry and give gluten time to relax. Dough that springs back aggressively during shaping often needs more rest, not more dry flour.

Bench flour accumulates

Heavy dusting makes shaping easy but leaves dry flour on the surface. That flour cooks into hard patches. Use only enough to prevent sticking and brush off obvious excess before the naan reaches the hot surface.

Cook fast, not forever

Tandoor-style naan relies on intense heat. At home, preheat the cooking surface thoroughly and stop once the bread has puffed in places and developed browned spots. If you keep it on moderate heat for many minutes, moisture leaves faster than the surface colours.

Protect cooked naan immediately

Stack finished naan and wrap loosely in a clean cloth or place in a covered bread basket so some steam remains. Do not seal dripping-hot bread in plastic, where condensation can make one side wet.

Reheating

Sprinkle or brush a very small amount of water, then reheat briefly on a hot tawa or in a covered pan. Long microwave heating makes bread temporarily soft but often tougher after it cools.
